Shore Check

California posts shellfish advisories after toxins are found in the water. NOAA forecasts domoic acid risk days ahead. Shore Check puts the two on one map, so you can see where an advisory is already in force and where the forecast says trouble may be coming.

Role
Design, data pipeline, web app
Tools
JavaScript, Leaflet, Python, GitHub Actions
Extent
California coast
Status
Live prototype

Open the live app   Code on GitHub

The question

Anyone gathering mussels, clams or oysters on the California coast is meant to check the state's shellfish advisories first. Those advisories are official and reliable, but they follow testing: an advisory appears after toxin has been measured in shellfish. A forecast looks the other way, at the ocean conditions that tend to come first. Shore Check asks what you gain by reading the two together.

What it shows

Pick a spot and the app reports, species by species, whether a California Department of Public Health advisory covers it, the history of advisories for that area, and a four-day C-HARM outlook for the nearest ocean cell, with the past 14 days of the C-HARM nowcast beside it. The official status and the forecast are kept visibly separate, because they are different kinds of information.

Read this first

Shore Check is a prototype and is never a statement that shellfish are safe. C-HARM is a research forecast, and its licence states it is not intended for legal use. Always confirm with CDPH before harvesting.

Data

Advisories. The map reads the web map behind CDPH's Recreational Shellfish Advisory Map and works out which areas are under which advisory. CDPH stores this as layer filters rather than as a feed, so this part of the pipeline is the most fragile: if CDPH rebuilds its map, the parser has to change. The checker reports when it can no longer read the map.

Forecast. C-HARM v3.1, from NOAA CoastWatch ERDDAP, on a 3 km grid updated daily. The nowcast and the one-, two- and three-day forecasts give probabilities from 0 to 1 for particulate domoic acid, cellular domoic acid and Pseudo-nitzschia.

Basemap and boundaries. Esri hillshade and dark gray canvas, with county shapes from CDPH's coastal counties service.

The checker

The advisory feed has no history, so I built one. A short Python script runs every three hours on GitHub Actions and does three things:

  • Compares the advisories in force with the last run and logs each one as issued or lifted.
  • Saves the C-HARM nowcast and forecasts at nine long-running pier sampling sites, Trinidad to Scripps, to a daily record.
  • Raises an alert when an advisory changes, when a pier's nowcast crosses 50%, or when the CDPH map cannot be read.

The record starts on 5 October 2026, so it is short. CDPH does not publish start dates, so advisories already in force on that day are logged as active when the record began.

Why it matters for research

The interesting gap is between "no advisory" and "high forecast". In early October 2026, Santa Cruz had no bivalve advisory while C-HARM showed roughly 77% probability just offshore. How often do high C-HARM values come before a CDPH advisory, and by how many days? The daily record is collecting the evidence to answer that, and because the sites are long-running pier sampling stations, the forecast can eventually be checked against measured samples as well.

Limits

The record is weeks old, not years, so it cannot yet say anything about forecast skill. C-HARM is a model and its grid is coarse near the shore. The advisory parser depends on a map structure CDPH could change without notice. An official machine-readable feed from CDPH would fix that, and asking for one is the obvious next step.
