These come out of coursework, but each one is a study area I picked and a question I framed. What ties them together is less the subject matter than a habit: the interesting part of a remote sensing result is usually the thing that could make it wrong — a display stretch inherited from a different sensor, a validation set too small to support its own decimal places, a city that swapped out every streetlight in the middle of the study period.

Classifying a coastline, and checking whether it worked

Florida Keys & Everglades · Sentinel-2

A five-year Sentinel-2 median composite over south Florida, classified into developed, herbaceous, water, and land. Cloud is handled in two tiers — a scene-level filter throws out bad acquisitions, then a per-pixel cloud-probability mask removes what survives inside the good ones. Neither alone is enough over a subtropical marine area.

The same scene is classified twice: k-means with seven clusters, which knows nothing about the categories, and Random Forest with four trained classes. Asking the unsupervised method for more groups than the scheme has is deliberate — the extra clusters split water by depth, because in clear shallow water the bottom drives reflectance. That is real physical structure the four-class scheme throws away.

Validation was an 80/20 split and a confusion matrix: 95.8% overall accuracy, kappa 0.94. The more useful number is underneath it. Water and developed classified perfectly; the single error was land misread as herbaceous — and land had 12 training points against water's 52. Difficulty concentrated in the vegetated classes and tracked sample size. The headline figure also rests on 24 validation samples drawn from the same polygons as the training data, so it is an upper bound rather than a measurement.

Reading a thermal archive without misreading it

Spain · MODIS LST + Pathfinder SST

MODIS land surface temperature paired with sea surface temperature offshore, November 2022. Both are stored as scaled integers rather than physical values, which is where this kind of analysis usually goes wrong.

The first display range, 13000–16500 raw, works out to −13 °C to +57 °C — a seventy-degree span for Iberia in November, so almost the whole map rendered as one undifferentiated mid-ramp colour. Reading the histogram first gave 13800–15500, or 3 °C to 37 °C, which is the actual distribution. Same image, same palette; the only thing that changed was looking at the data before choosing how to show it.

The second half is vector work: buffering the coastline 100 km offshore and intersecting it with an area of interest to build a coastal zone and measure it — structurally the same operation as delineating a jurisdictional band for marine spatial planning.

Cloud as the measurement

Anchorage, Phoenix, Los Angeles · Landsat 8

Every other study here treats cloud as contamination. This one inverts that: a per-image cloud likelihood score becomes the variable, aggregated into an annual time series for three cities with very different climates.

The result is a lesson about the metric more than about the cities. The cloud-scoring algorithm keys on brightness, coolness, and a snow index — so it cannot separate cloud from snow. Anchorage carries snow much of the year and scores high partly for that reason, which flatters the hypothesis it was meant to test. The scoring region also defaults to the full Landsat footprint, roughly 185 km across, so these describe the region containing each city rather than the city.

The second half puts a number on what cloud costs you, differencing cloud-filtered against unfiltered mean NDVI. The difference runs broadly negative, which is the opposite of the naive expectation — and the likely reason is a sampling bias, not a radiometric one. In a maritime subarctic climate the clear days are disproportionately cold, snow-covered ones, so filtering for low cloud quietly filters for winter.

Two ways to detect change

South Miami-Dade & the Lower Keys · NLCD, Landsat 5 & 8

The same eighteen years read categorically and continuously. The first groups NLCD classes and finds gains and losses through boolean comparison; the second differences NDVI between a 2001 Landsat 5 composite and a 2019 Landsat 8 one, which catches gradual thinning that never crosses a class boundary.

The first run produced a great deal of apparent development loss, which is physically implausible in Miami. The cause was the class grouping: using deciduous forest alone meant an evergreen stand registered as forest loss purely for not being deciduous. Grouping all three forest classes and both agricultural classes removed most of the spurious change. The error was in the categories, not the data.

The NDVI half carries its own caveat, which the write-up keeps rather than hides: the quality mask uses Collection 1 bit positions against Collection 2 data, so it removes cloud and snow but lets cloud shadow through — and shadow depresses NDVI, which reads as vegetation loss. Landsat 5 and 8 also have different spectral response functions, so their NDVI values are not strictly comparable without harmonisation.

Is the city actually getting brighter?

Santa Clara County, CA · VIIRS 2016 vs 2023

Annual VIIRS nighttime lights composites for the South Bay, differenced across seven years, with water masked and the change split into gain and loss by area. The arithmetic says 83.3% of the area brightened and 16.7% dimmed.

That number should not be read as "the South Bay got 83% brighter," for two reasons the map makes visible.

San José replaced every streetlight in the city with LED, finishing in early 2023 — inside this exact window. The VIIRS Day/Night Band is comparatively insensitive to the blue-rich output of LEDs, while the sodium lamps they replaced emitted strongly where the sensor is most responsive. A converted street therefore reads dimmer even when it is lit as brightly or brighter. Look at where the pink sits: it follows the street grid, exactly where streetlights dominate the signal.

And the percentage is a sign test with no magnitude threshold — a dark hillside pixel drifting from 0.04 to 0.05 nW counts the same as downtown moving from 60 to 70. Much of this area is unlit hills where the year-to-year difference is mostly sensor noise. The defensible reading is that lit area expanded in extent, while the lit core dimmed for reasons that are partly about lamps rather than activity.
