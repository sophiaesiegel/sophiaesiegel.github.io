Siting Wind in California

What actually makes a good wind farm site — wind resource, terrain, and access to grid that can carry the power — worked into a siting model and rendered as a 3D scene where the transmission network stands at a height set by its voltage.

Role
Analysis, 3D visualisation
Tools
ArcGIS Pro, Local Scene
Extent
California, NAD 1983 Teale Albers
Output
Siting workflow + 3D scene

The question

If you have driven east on the I-10 you have passed through a forest of turbines. The intuitive explanation for why they are there is that it is windy — but that is incomplete, and in places wrong. Turbines appear on ridgelines as often as on flats. Consistency of wind may matter more than peak speed. And a site with an excellent wind resource is worthless if connecting it means building thirty miles of new high-voltage line.

Working backwards from what exists

Rather than guessing at thresholds, the analysis starts from the installed fleet. Every existing California turbine becomes a sample point, and each environmental layer is read off at those locations — turning the built fleet into an empirical record of what developers have actually judged acceptable. The criteria fall out of the distributions instead of being asserted up front.

Extract Values to Points pulls the raster layers onto the turbines:

  • Elevation — from the California DEM
  • Slope — derived from that DEM, in degrees
  • Wind speed — Global Wind Atlas, developed by the World Bank and DTU Energy
  • Nighttime lights — VIIRS, a proxy for where load and infrastructure already sit

Grid access needs a different tool, because it is a distance rather than a value at a location. The Near function measures each turbine to the closest transmission line carrying more than 115 kV, written into a dist_power_lines_miles field.

Each variable then goes to a histogram, and the histograms are the actual output of this stage. A mean distance to high-voltage line tells you what interconnection distance the industry has tolerated in practice; a slope distribution tells you how steep is too steep. Splitting the elevation histogram by year adds a second question on top — whether the elevations being built on have drifted over time as the easy sites were taken.

Why voltage is the filter

Turbines generate at roughly 690 V to 34.5 kV — far too low to move power any distance. A step-up transformer at the farm lifts it to transmission levels of 69 kV to 500 kV before it travels to a substation and is stepped back down for delivery.

So proximity to high-voltage line is not a refinement, it is the constraint. Filtering transmission to above 115 kV rather than treating all lines alike is the difference between a model that reflects interconnection economics and one that merely looks like it does.

3D as analysis, not decoration

The scene renders wind speed as a continuous surface from 0 to about 12 m/s, with turbines as 3D symbols and the transmission network extruded so that line height encodes voltage — the backbone standing visibly above the lower-voltage network.

That encoding is doing real work. On a flat map, voltage becomes a colour or a line weight, competing with terrain, wind, and everything else for the reader's attention. Given height, the transmission hierarchy becomes structural: the question a siting analyst actually asks — is this location near grid that can take the output — resolves visually, in one look, instead of requiring a legend lookup.

The wind surface is symbolised on a high-contrast ramp rather than a naturalistic one, for the same reason. The point of the base layer is to make gradients in the resource legible underneath the infrastructure, not to look like terrain.

Workflow

  1. Assemble California border, elevation, transmission lines, and turbine points; reproject to NAD 1983 (2011) California Teale Albers.
  2. Filter transmission to lines above 115 kV.
  3. Add Global Wind Atlas wind speed and VIIRS nighttime lights, clipping the global rasters to the California outline at the map level rather than by raster extraction — these datasets are too large and fine-grained to clip conventionally.
  4. Derive slope from the DEM, then Extract Values to Points for elevation, slope, and wind speed at every turbine.
  5. Run Near to get each turbine's distance to the nearest >115 kV line, and plot the distributions.
  6. Build the scene: wind speed as the base surface with vertical exaggeration on the ground, turbines as 3D symbols, transmission extruded by voltage.

From description to a siting model

Everything above characterises sites that already exist. Turning that into a search for new ones needs a spatial frame, because suitability has to be evaluated somewhere rather than at points that are already built on.

  1. Divide the state into a tessellation — equal hexagons or squares — so every candidate location is a comparable unit.
  2. Remove what is disqualified outright: built-up areas, protected land, anything unavailable regardless of resource.
  3. Score the surviving cells on the variables the histograms identified as mattering — wind speed, slope, elevation, distance to high-voltage line.
  4. Rescale each to a common 0–1 range and weight them by importance, since raw metres, degrees, m/s, and miles cannot be combined directly.
  5. Combine into a single suitability surface.

That final step is the same fuzzy-overlay logic used in the ocean carbon removal work — different domain, identical problem: several incommensurable variables that have to be argued onto one scale before they can be combined.

The fire question

Wind corridors and fire country overlap in California, and transmission infrastructure is both vulnerable to wildfire and occasionally a cause of it. Buffering the turbines by five miles and intersecting that with the high-risk classes from the emerging hot spot analysis — intensifying, persistent, and new — counts how much of the installed fleet sits inside a fire regime that is active or getting worse.

It reframes the siting question. A location can score well on wind, terrain, and grid access and still be a poor bet over an asset life measured in decades, because the hazard around it is on a trajectory the resource assessment never looks at.

What the model leaves out

Land cover, land ownership, distance to roads and urban areas, and proximity to protected areas all belong in a real siting decision and are not in this one. Naming those absences is part of the deliverable: a suitability model that does not declare its own boundaries invites being read as more authoritative than it is.
