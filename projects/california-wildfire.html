Where California's Fire Risk Is Going

Not where California has burned — where its fire regime is changing. An emerging hot spot analysis built on a space-time cube, separating places that are intensifying from places that burned early in the record and have since gone quiet.

Role
Analysis, cartography
Tools
ArcGIS Pro, Space Time Cube
Extent
California, Teale Albers
Unit
Space-time bins + county summary

The question

California is a fire-shaped landscape. Chaparral, oak woodland, and Sierra conifer forest all evolved with frequent, mostly low- to moderate-intensity burning — much of it set deliberately by Indigenous communities over millennia to manage fuel and maintain ecosystem structure. A century of aggressive suppression interrupted that rhythm. Fuels accumulated just as the climate warmed and dried and the wildland-urban interface expanded into them.

The usual response is a map of where fire has occurred. That map is easy to make and nearly useless for planning, because it treats a burn from the start of the record and a burn from last season as the same fact. The question worth asking is directional: which parts of the state are on a trajectory, and which are not?

Why a space-time cube

Method note

A conventional hot spot analysis collapses time. It answers "where is fire clustered" and cannot distinguish a place with a long, steady history from one that has caught fire only recently but is accelerating.

A space-time cube keeps both axes. Fire events are aggregated into bins that are simultaneously spatial and temporal — a grid of columns, each column sliced into time steps. Running hot spot statistics through that structure yields not one answer but a classification of trajectories.

That classification is what the map's colours encode. The categories that matter most for planning are the ones that separate direction from magnitude:

  • New — the most recent time step is a hot spot; earlier ones were not
  • Consecutive / intensifying — an unbroken recent run of hot spots, with clustering growing stronger over time
  • Persistent — hot throughout, with no meaningful trend either way
  • Diminishing / historical — significant clustering in the past that has weakened or lapsed

A "historical" county and an "intensifying" county can have identical total burned area. They call for entirely different responses, and a map that only shows accumulated footprint cannot tell them apart.

Reading the layout

  1. The main map carries the hot spot classification across the state, drawn on a dark basemap so the categorical colours carry the signal rather than competing with terrain.
  2. The county bar chart summarises hot spot hazard counts per county, turning a continuous surface into something rankable — the northern counties of Siskiyou, Trinity, and Shasta stand out, alongside Monterey, Mendocino, and Riverside.
  3. The 3D inset takes Monterey County and shows the cube itself: columns rising through time, each block coloured by its state in that time step, so a single county's trajectory is legible as a shape.

Cartography on a dark ground

The dark basemap is a functional choice, not a stylistic one. The hot spot output is categorical — the colours mean distinct classes, not positions on a ramp — and categorical schemes need high separation to stay readable. Against a pale terrain basemap the lighter classes wash out and the reader starts inferring an ordering that the data does not support.

The grid cells also keep a soft outer glow, which does real work at small sizes: it holds isolated single cells visible against the dark ground instead of letting them disappear, so sparse emerging clusters read as clearly as dense ones.

Putting the classification to work

The categories are useful precisely because they can be intersected with something else. Treating intensifying, persistent, and new hot spots together as "high risk" and buffering assets against them turns a descriptive map into an exposure count — which is how this analysis feeds the wind siting project, where it answers how much of California's installed turbine fleet sits within five miles of a fire regime that is active or worsening.

What this map does not say

Emerging hot spot categories describe the statistical behaviour of the record, not causation. A county classified as intensifying is not thereby explained — the driver could be fuel accumulation, climate, ignition sources, changes in detection and reporting, or a shifting wildland-urban boundary. The analysis narrows where to look. It does not substitute for looking.
