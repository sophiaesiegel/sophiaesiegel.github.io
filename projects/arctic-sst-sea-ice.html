Arctic Sea Ice Decline with SST

Sea surface temperature and Arctic sea ice extent, drawn together — a polar global scene with the two records running as time series beneath it.

Role
Analysis, cartography
Tools
Google Earth Engine, ArcGIS Pro
Extent
Arctic, EPSG:3413
Period
2001–2025

The question

Arctic sea ice loss is one of the most widely reproduced climate graphics there is, and usually it appears alone — a line going down. That framing leaves out the ocean underneath. This project puts the two records in the same frame: the temperature of the surface water and the ice sitting on it, over the same period, in the same projection.

Data

  • Sea surface temperature — NOAA Optimum Interpolation SST v2.1 (NOAA/CDR/OISST/V2_1) via Google Earth Engine. The sst band is stored as scaled integers and needs multiplying by 0.01 to reach degrees Celsius. Reduced to annual means and exported as a multiband GeoTIFF with one band per year, reprojected to EPSG:3413, the polar stereographic projection standard for Arctic work.
  • Sea ice extent — a monthly polygon feature class carrying year, month, area, and extent fields, joined by the supporting cartographic layers that give a polar map its bearings: the Arctic Circle, world countries, a graticule, and northern marine regions.

Cartography

The layout runs on a black ground with the globe lit from behind, which is not a stylistic flourish so much as the only way to get two quantitative surfaces into one frame. Sea ice extent is drawn on a white-to-blue ramp and SST on a blue-to-orange diverging ramp; on a pale basemap the ice would vanish into the land and the cool end of the SST ramp would fight the ice for the same range. Darkness separates them.

Blend modes let the ice sit over the temperature field without a hard outline, so the two layers read as one surface rather than a stack. The graticule and the Arctic Circle — the red ring — are held deliberately thin: at the pole the meridians converge into a dense knot exactly where the subject matter is, and anything heavier would obscure it.

Choosing the right number for ice

Analysis note

Annual mean ice extent is the wrong summary for a melt story. It blends the winter maximum back in and damps out exactly the signal of interest. The annual minimum — the September low — is what actually tracks long-term loss, so that is the value plotted.

Taking a minimum across months has a trap in it: any year with incomplete monthly coverage returns a spuriously low value that looks like a record melt. Filtering to years with a full twelve months of records first removes the partial years at either end of the series and keeps the trend honest.

Building the layout

  1. Compute annual mean SST in Earth Engine and export as a multiband GeoTIFF, band per year.
  2. Bring the raster into ArcGIS Pro and set up a Global Scene centred on the pole, so the Arctic reads as a cap on a sphere rather than a distorted rectangle — which is the honest way to show a region that every flat projection wrecks.
  3. Summarise the ice feature class to annual minimum extent, dropping partial years.
  4. Chart both series beneath the scene and assemble the layout.

What the chart shows

Across 2001 to 2025 the minimum extent bars fall from roughly 6.8 to around 4.5 million km², with the well-known 2012 collapse showing as the deepest single trough in the record. The mean SST line rises across the same window by a few tenths of a degree.

The two series are deliberately not presented as a causal pair. Half a degree of globally averaged surface warming does not mechanically produce several million square kilometres of ice loss; the relationship runs through albedo feedback, ocean heat transport, and atmospheric circulation, none of which appear on this map. What the layout supports is the weaker and defensible claim — that the two records move together over the same period.

Two units, one chart

Ice extent in millions of square kilometres and SST in degrees Celsius share no sensible axis, and putting them on one flattens whichever series has the narrower range — here that would be SST, whose entire signal spans a few tenths of a degree and would collapse to a flat line beside bars running from zero to seven.

The chart solves it with independent left and right axes: extent on the left driving the bars, mean SST on the right driving the line, each scaled to its own range over a shared timeline. The alternative — normalising both to a common index — was available but discards the real units, and the units are worth keeping when a reader wants to know that the minimum is around four and a half million km² rather than simply "low".

The cost of a dual axis is that it can imply a relationship the data does not establish, since the apparent convergence of two lines depends entirely on how each axis was scaled. That is a real hazard here, which is why the causal claim is left explicitly unmade above.

Reusable pattern

This project established the Earth Engine → ArcGIS Pro pipeline that the ocean carbon work now reuses: reduce a global archive to annual means in the cloud where the data already lives, export a single multiband raster rather than a folder of files, and do the cartography locally where the layout tools are.
